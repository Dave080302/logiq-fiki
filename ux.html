02 / ONE MAIN PERSONA

The reader who already checks.
But needs a clearer route.

RESEARCH-INFORMED COMPOSITE · PROVISIONAL

Alex, the careful news reader

“Help me understand what is supported, without spending my evening chasing links.”

Proposed initial segment: students and early-career professionals who read online news and manually investigate important or questionable claims.

Alex is a fictional composite informed mainly by Baldo and Georgiana. No exact age, income, or personal biography is asserted. The fit and size of this customer segment remain unvalidated.

CONTEXT, GOAL & FRICTION

Checking while reading

  • Context: reading a browser article or receiving a link in a conversation.
  • Goal: decide whether a particular claim is reliable enough to act on or share.
  • Current behavior: search, compare coverage, inspect official sources, ask knowledgeable people.
  • Friction: unclear terms, missing context, repeated stories, and the time required to trace evidence.
  • Needs: quick entry, understandable explanations, identifiable sources, and honest uncertainty.

The older Elena and Andrei personas inspired accessible language and optional depth. Elena remains a secondary hypothesis: the current evidence does not establish the needs of all older or less digitally confident users. The original Google documents and slides remain unchanged.

04 / FLOWS & USE CASES

A small check.
A choice at every step.

  1. Main use case — assess a news claim: Alex opens an article, requests a check, examines an individual claim, reads the source details, and returns to reading. Success means understanding the evidence and its limits.
  2. Alternative entry — a shared link: Alex enters a URL or text. Empty input and the unconnected live-analysis state have explicit responses.
  3. Alternative outcome — unresolved: the available pack is insufficient; Alex sees what information is missing and can leave without a forced verdict.
  4. Exception — unavailable article: no claim assessment is produced. Alex can try the text entry point or another accessible sample.
  5. Optional branches: share a sourced summary or preview feedback. Neither is required to close the panel or return to reading.

Design decisions

  • Evidence before a verdict: assessments belong to specific factual claims. Emotional language or a weak argument alone does not establish falsity.
  • Accessible labels: supported, contradicted, missing context, and insufficient evidence use text as well as color.
  • User control: check on request, close at any time, and retain simple routes back to reading. Phone layout stacks the experience rather than implying unrestricted access to other apps.
  • Progressive detail: a compact overview leads to claim-level explanations, optional context, and source details.
  • Visible scope: fictional articles and curated evidence are labeled once in the persistent ribbon and again where copied content could leave the demo.

Implementation boundary

The M4 site is a static interactive prototype. It does not install a browser extension, read a real page, retrieve live evidence, analyze arbitrary text, bypass restricted content, detect deepfakes, create accounts, or collect feedback. External source links open the original pages. The proposed product can eventually use those capabilities where feasible; they are not demonstrated as working here.

The earlier Google references and Figma Make project are left intact. The new Figma file is a separate editable design companion. The interactive website is the version to use for functionality feedback.

06 / READY FOR YOUR NEXT INTERVIEWS

Let people try it.
Then let the findings lead.

Use these tasks after a short conversation about the participant’s recent news-checking behavior. Ask them to think aloud. Let them explore before explaining where to click.

  1. Check the phone-repair story before sharing it.

    Observe whether they find the check action and distinguish the two claim assessments. Ask: “What would you take away from this result?”

  2. Find the evidence behind one assessment.

    Observe whether they reach source details and can identify the publisher and relevant information. Ask: “What makes this source useful—or not useful—to you?”

  3. Review the solar headline.

    Ask what the headline leaves out. Observe whether “missing context” is understood differently from “false”.

  4. Decide what to do with the bus announcement.

    Observe whether “insufficient evidence” leads to an appropriate next check rather than assumed falsity.

  5. Bring a shared link, then try the inaccessible article.

    Observe input errors and recovery. Ask: “What did you expect to happen here?” Explain the prototype’s live-analysis limit if it becomes relevant.

  6. Prepare a summary for a friend, then challenge a result.

    Observe copy, fallback, feedback selection, and returning to the article. Ask which parts they would want included when sharing.

Questions after the tasks

  • “Tell me about a recent situation in which this would—or would not—have helped.”
  • “What did the different result labels mean to you?”
  • “What would you need to see before trusting an assessment?”
  • “What felt unclear, unnecessary, or too much work?”
  • “Would you prefer a browser panel, a separate app, or another way to start? Why?”
