02 / TWO PERSONAS

Two readers.
Different starting points.

MAIN PERSONA

Alex, the careful news reader

“Help me understand what is supported, without spending my evening chasing links.”

Profile: a student or early-career professional who reads online news and already checks important or questionable claims.

Alex is comfortable searching and comparing sources, but wants a quicker way to understand conflicting coverage and missing context.

ALEX / CONTEXT, GOAL & FRICTION

A quicker route to the evidence

  • Context: browser articles and social feeds, on a laptop or phone.
  • Goal: decide whether a particular claim is reliable enough to act on or share.
  • Current behavior: search, compare coverage, inspect official sources and ask knowledgeable people.
  • Friction: unclear terms, incomplete information and time spent tracing the original evidence.
  • Needs from LogiQ: a quiet alert, claim-level explanations, identifiable sources and visible uncertainty.

SECONDARY PERSONA

Elena, the everyday news reader

“If someone I trust sends it, I tend to believe it. Help me know when to pause.”

Profile: 64, retired, comfortable using her smartphone for messages and social media, with little experience checking online news.

Elena often trusts familiar contacts and official-looking headlines. She may miss an old publication date or confuse a repost with an original source. Urgent, emotional messages can make her act or forward a story before checking it.

ELENA / CONTEXT, GOAL & FRICTION

A clear reason to pause

  • Context: news encountered in a phone feed or opened from a family group message.
  • Goal: stay informed and protect her family from misleading information.
  • Current behavior: rely on the sender’s familiarity, skim the headline and occasionally ask a relative for help.
  • Friction: difficulty identifying the original publisher, checking dates and understanding technical language.
  • Needs from LogiQ: readable text, a recognizable and calm notification, short everyday explanations, clear publisher and date labels, and an obvious way back to reading.

Example scenario: a relative forwards the bus announcement. Elena opens it on her phone, taps the LogiQ notification and sees that the date and transport operator are missing. She looks for the original announcement before forwarding it.

These are fictional design profiles. Elena’s habits and needs are hypotheses to explore in follow-up interviews.

04 / FLOWS & USE CASES

Keep reading.
Open a second perspective.

  1. Read: Alex reads an article or a shared story in a feed. The simulated review starts automatically.
  2. Notice: a distinctly branded LogiQ popup flags a potentially misleading claim, missing context, or missing source. Alex can dismiss it and continue.
  3. Expand: one click or tap opens findings, explanations and expandable sources together.
  4. Continue: closing returns to the reading position. The small LogiQ Q button can reopen the completed review.

The same flow applies to all four contexts below. The three sample stories cover a contradicted claim, missing context and insufficient evidence. An unresolved claim is not labeled false.

Design decisions to test

  • Recognizable ownership: the muted Q mark and LogiQ label separate the alert from article and feed content.
  • Low interruption: no forced modal, no automatic focus change, no repeated alert after dismissal.
  • Equal depth on mobile: the same findings, sources and uncertainty remain available on a phone.
  • One expanded view: claim explanations and sources use accordions instead of separate pages.
  • Reader control: close, dismiss and reopen are available without completing any other task.

The prototype uses fictional content and curated evidence. Automatic detection is simulated; no real page is read or analyzed.

06 / INTERVIEW & FEEDBACK GUIDE

Let people try it.
Then let the findings lead.

Begin with the participant’s recent news-checking behavior. Ask them to think aloud, then give tasks without pointing out the alert. Rotate the starting context across sessions so one device does not always benefit from prior learning.

  1. Read the phone-repair article on desktop.

    Observe whether they notice the automatic alert and identify it as LogiQ. Ask: “Who do you think this message is from? What would you do next?”

  2. Continue with the browser story on mobile.

    Ask them to understand the issue and inspect the original source. Observe tap targets, scrolling, source comprehension and closing back to the article.

  3. Browse the shared story in a desktop feed.

    Observe whether the alert is distinguished from a post, ad or platform notification. Ask them to dismiss it, keep reading and reopen the findings.

  4. Review the solar story in a mobile feed.

    Observe whether the missing-context explanation is understood and sources are discoverable without extra pages. Ask: “What does the headline leave out?”

  5. Decide what to do with the bus announcement.

    Use the participant’s preferred device. Ask what they would check next. Observe whether insufficient evidence is mistaken for a false verdict.

Questions after the tasks

  • “When was the alert helpful, distracting or easy to miss?”
  • “What made it clear—or unclear—that LogiQ was separate from the page?”
  • “Which parts felt easier on your phone or computer?”
  • “What would you need before trusting an assessment?”
  • “Tell me about a recent situation where this would have helped.”
